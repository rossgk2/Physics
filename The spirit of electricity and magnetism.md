# The spirit of electricity and magnetism

Classical mechanics can be understood by making extremely elementary observations about the world and then interpreting the world in what feels like a very spiritual way. “There is a currency of motion. *Force* is that which changes it.”

Though I didn’t realize that that this sort of spiritual theme was what I felt presentations of electricity and magnetism lacked, I was always looking for it.

At first, I thought the elegant alternate characterizations of electricity and magnetism (like Landau and Lifshitz’s derivation of electricity and magnetism from special relativity) were what I was looking for. But then I realized that it is not really reasonable to expect that someone could divine the hidden assumptions of such alternate characterizations without  already having a very good idea of how reality works. This made me feel unmoored for a time, like I was floating in a space of theoretical equivocations.

Then I realized that there is only one way to correctly cherry-pick the actual axioms of our reality from the infinite set of reasonable-sounding axioms: look at our reality and see what happens. Observations, which used to be tedium I barely tolerated as stepping stones on the way to theory, now became shining lights; unique, mysterious, miraculous keys to *our* world that simply *are*.

This realization, a result of chasing the spirit of electricity and magnetism, surprisingly changed my understanding of classical mechanics. I now saw that my understanding of classical mechanics had fallen prey to the trap that even textbooks as good as Vladimir Arnold’s beloved *Mathematical Methods of Classical Mechanics* do, which is to think that the definition **F** = m**a** is relatively arbitrary, and that everything else can be derived from it. Now, I knew that something cannot be derived from nothing, and I began figuring out what the axioms underlying classical mechanics were. The central one is, as I alluded to above, the axiom of conservation of momentum. Put spiritually, it says: “There is a currency of motion.”

Poetic beauty like this is typical of classical mechanics. In electricity and magnetism, however, the necessary initial observations are so eccentric that any single individual could only stumble on them as a result of chance. And this is only at first! As one takes more steps and progresses further, technological apparatuses- with designs is predicated on theory that could only be reasonably discovered from eccentric observations- become necessary to obtain more observations.

Thus, understanding physics thus entails understanding not only which *observations* were most reasonable to stumble on, not only the most insightful *theories* could reasonably follow them, but also how the theories enabled using the *means of the time* to produce more advanced *technology* that could be used to collect more *observations*. From what I can tell, us humans historically tended to make the most fruitful observations that were reasonable to make, and to extract the most insight out of those observations that was reasonable to extract. This brings us to my present view: *understanding physics well entails constructing an elegantly framed narrative of the history of physics*.

Notice that I specified a narrative of the history of physics, not the raw history itself. This is because understanding mathematics is not nearly as dependent on understanding history as is physics. Thus, we can restructure the raw narrative of history into a narrative of the best possible inferences we would have been able to make if we had applied modern mathematical insights. And, there are *some* times when we arguably could have been more efficient or clever than we were with the means of the time. The best understanding, the best narrative, adjusts for those times, too.
